Repair Bench

Repair guides

Not every old machine needs new capacitors. Some need a fresh battery, a new belt, a clean cartridge slot or a laser that has been adjusted. Pick a repair for the steps, parts, tools and cautions, plus a checklist that saves.

Computers and gear

Battery

  • Check the internal battery in a Roland Sound Canvas (Easy, about 30 minutes). Sound Canvas modules carry a small lithium battery that can leak and damage the board over time. Opening the unit, checking and removing the battery is a short job that can save the board.
  • Clean up an Acorn Archimedes battery leak (Careful, about 100 minutes). A buyer's guide for Archimedes machines calls battery leaks common, and a repair write-up shows how bad it can get on an A5000. Remove the battery, clean the area and check traces before powering the machine up.
  • Deal with a leaked CMOS or RTC battery on an old PC board (Careful, about 90 minutes). 286 to early Pentium PCs often carry a barrel-style rechargeable cell or a soldered lithium cell that has since died or leaked onto the board. Clean the residue before it spreads, then fit a battery arrangement that is easy to swap.
  • Remove a leaking Amiga clock battery and clean the board (Careful, about 90 minutes). Old clock batteries on Amiga boards are known to leak and eat traces, so the safest repair is to take the battery off the board entirely and clean up. This guide covers removing it, cleaning the damage, checking traces and fitting a replacement that cannot leak onto the board.
  • Replace the Mac PRAM battery and clean up a leak (Medium, about 60 minutes). A flat PRAM battery makes older Macs forget their settings, and a leaking one can damage the logic board. Replacing it and cleaning any residue early is among the cheapest ways to protect a compact Mac or a II-series.
  • Revive or replace the battery in a vintage laptop (Careful, about 120 minutes). Many early laptops depend on a sealed rechargeable pack plus a smaller backup battery, and both age badly. This guide covers testing, replacing the backup cell and deciding whether to recell or replace the main pack.

Case

  • Reduce yellowing on vintage computer plastic (retrobright) (Careful, about 240 minutes). Some old ABS plastics yellow because of a flame retardant that reacts with UV light. Retrobright treatments use peroxide and UV light to bring the colour back, but results vary and the chemicals demand care.
  • Repair a cracked case or a broken screw post (Medium, about 90 minutes). Old plastic gets brittle, and cracked tabs and posts are common. With the right glue and a little patience many breaks can be repaired invisibly or reinforced from the back.

Cleaning

  • Clean a ball mouse that skips or sticks (Easy, about 20 minutes). Dirt builds up on the little rollers inside a ball mouse and makes the pointer behave oddly. A clean takes minutes and needs no parts.
  • Clean and fix a worn joystick (Easy, about 45 minutes). Dirty or worn switches and potentiometers cause most joystick faults. Opening it, cleaning the contacts and replacing worn microswitches often fixes the problem.
  • Replace dried thermal paste and noisy fans (Medium, about 60 minutes). Thermal paste dries out and fans clog or wear after many years. Cleaning, fresh paste and a new fan are cheap and bring temperatures down.

Diagnosis

  • Diagnose a blank screen on an 8-bit home computer (Medium, about 60 minutes). A blank screen is often the power supply, the cable or a loose chip rather than the whole board. Work from outside to inside and keep notes.
  • Read PC POST beeps and fix a no-video start (Easy, about 30 minutes). The motherboard BIOS beeps in patterns to say what failed. Note the pattern and BIOS vendor, then check the likely culprits such as RAM, video card and power.
  • Read the Amiga power-on screen colours and find a dead-boot fault (Careful, about 60 minutes). An Amiga uses the screen colour at power-on to hint where a fault lies. Note the colour, then compare it with a published chart for your model.
  • Work out why a classic Mac will not start (chimes, sad Mac, question mark) (Medium, about 45 minutes). A flashing question mark usually means no startup disk was found, while a sad Mac or unusual chimes point at hardware such as RAM or logic board. Work through the cheap checks before opening the case.

Display

  • Check a CRT monitor that shows nothing, while staying safe (Careful, about 45 minutes). Many CRT faults are the cable, the source or a setting, so work through the outside checks first. Anything past the case needs stored-voltage knowledge, so stop and hire a professional.
  • Work out why an LCD or laptop screen is dark or dim (Careful, about 60 minutes). A faint picture usually means the backlight, its inverter or its cable has failed, not the LCD itself. Narrowing it down takes a few outside checks and a torch.

Power

  • Replace a dead vintage PC power supply with an ATX unit (Careful, about 90 minutes). Original AT and XT supplies are hard to find and often need serious work. Many owners swap the whole unit for a modern supply, or a compact unit like a Meanwell, and keep the original for display.
  • Replace a failing Commodore 64 power brick (Easy, about 20 minutes). The original sealed C64 power brick is widely reported as a weak point, and a failing one is said to be able to feed the computer bad voltages. Replacing it with a modern unit made for the C64 protects the chips.

Drive

  • Check and adjust the head alignment on a Commodore 1541 (Expert, about 120 minutes). A 1541 can drift out of alignment, so disks written elsewhere fail to read. Alignment is delicate; use the official service manual and an alignment aid, and be prepared to give up and seek help.
  • Clean and lubricate a floppy drive that will not read (Medium, about 75 minutes). Dried grease and dust are a common reason an old floppy drive struggles. A careful clean of the heads and rails, with a tiny amount of fresh lubricant in the right places, often brings a drive back before any parts are replaced.
  • Coax a stuck vintage hard drive into spinning up (Expert, about 60 minutes). Old hard drives can seize when the heads stick to the platters or the spindle grease hardens. Before trying anything, work out whether the drive is worth saving, copy what you can off, and accept that the data may not survive.
  • Diagnose a Zip drive that clicks and ejects disks (Medium, about 45 minutes). The 'click of death' can come from a damaged cartridge or a worn drive, and a bad cartridge can damage a good drive. Isolate which one is at fault before using valuable disks.
  • Fit a Gotek floppy emulator to an Amiga (Medium, about 60 minutes). A Gotek swaps the original floppy drive for a unit that reads disk images from a USB stick. The usual route is to flash it with the free FlashFloppy firmware, set the jumpers for Amiga and mount it in place of the old drive.
  • Fit a Gotek to an Atari ST or a PC with a floppy port (Medium, about 60 minutes). A Gotek floppy emulator reads disk images from a USB stick and mimics the machine's floppy drive. The main differences from an Amiga install are the image format and the drive-select jumper.
  • Fix a CD-ROM drive with a bad tray belt or a dirty laser lens (Medium, about 60 minutes). Slow, weak tray movement is usually a perished belt, and read errors are often a dirty lens or worn pickup. A belt swap and a careful lens clean fix many CD-based drives without touching the electronics.
  • Get a Commodore Datasette turning and loading reliably (Medium, about 60 minutes). A tired belt, a dirty pinch roller and a misaligned head are the usual reasons a Datasette stops loading. Start with cleaning and a belt swap before touching head alignment.
  • Replace a dead SCSI hard drive with a SCSI2SD or ZuluSCSI (Medium, about 90 minutes). A SCSI2SD or ZuluSCSI board presents an SD card to the Mac as a SCSI drive. It is a practical way to bring a vintage SCSI Mac back to life without hunting for scarce old drives.
  • Replace a failing IDE hard drive with a CF or SD adapter (Easy, about 45 minutes). A CompactFlash or SD-to-IDE adapter lets a modern card replace a failing IDE disk in many older machines. It is silent, cool and usually a drop-in swap.
  • Replace a stretched or broken drive belt (Medium, about 60 minutes). Some old drives turn the spindle through a rubber belt that stretches or perishes with age. Replacing it is a mechanical job with a cheap part, once you have checked that your drive is a belt-driven model.

Keyboard

  • Deep-clean a sticky or dirty vintage keyboard (Medium, about 90 minutes). Removing the keycaps and washing the caps themselves while keeping the electronics dry can restore feel and reliability. The key mechanisms differ between models, so photograph the layout and follow a model-specific guide before removing anything beyond the keycaps.
  • Replace a dead ZX Spectrum keyboard membrane (Easy, about 30 minutes). Spectrum-family keyboards use a flat membrane with printed tracks that crack and wear. Replacing it is a short job that often cures dead keys.

Memory

  • Reseat RAM and cards, and clean dirty sockets and edge contacts (Easy, about 30 minutes). Oxidised contacts are a very common cause of RAM and card faults. Reseating modules and wiping contacts clean is cheap and fixes a surprising number of dead machines.
  • Reseat socketed chips and clean oxidised legs (Medium, about 60 minutes). Older machines use socketed chips whose legs oxidise and creep out. Reseating chips and cleaning the legs can bring a computer back with no soldering.

Consoles and handhelds

Battery

  • Replace Dreamcast VMU batteries (Easy, about 10 minutes). A VMU uses two coin cells that drain even when the unit sleeps, so a flat pair is the usual cause of a dead VMU.
  • Replace the battery in an N64 cartridge or Controller Pak (Medium, about 30 minutes). Fifteen N64 titles use a CR2032 to keep saves alive, and the Controller Pak uses the same cell. A fresh battery restores saving, but the old save may be lost during the swap.
  • Replace the Dreamcast clock battery (and the controller port fuse) (Careful, about 60 minutes). The clock battery sits on the controller board and is a rechargeable cell that eventually fails. While the board is out it is a good moment to check the F1 port fuse.
  • Replace the GameCube clock battery with a holder (Careful, about 45 minutes). The GameCube clock cell is soldered to the mainboard. Replacing it with a holder makes future changes easy.
  • Replace the Saturn clock and save battery (Easy, about 10 minutes). The Saturn keeps its clock and internal save memory alive with a CR2032 behind a rear panel. A flat cell is a cheap thing to fix.
  • Replace the save battery in a Game Boy, Game Boy Color or GBA cartridge (Medium, about 30 minutes). Saving games use a small coin cell on the cartridge board. When it dies, saves are lost, so swap it before it does.

Belt

  • Fix a Sega CD tray that will not move: belt, limiter switch and helix (Careful, about 90 minutes). The Model 1 Sega CD is known for bad tray belts, dirty limiter switches and worn helix screws. A belt swap and some cleaning fixes most tray problems.
  • Replace the Famicom Disk System drive belt and reset the head (Careful, about 90 minutes). The Disk System drive runs on a rubber belt that perishes with age, and the old one can even melt onto the motor pulley. A new belt and a head position reset usually brings it back.

Cartridge

  • Clean or replace the NES 72-pin cartridge connector (Medium, about 60 minutes). The front-loading NES connector wears, bends and corrodes, and it is the best-known reason these consoles stop booting. Cleaning fixes many units, and a new connector fixes the rest.
  • Fix an N64 black screen: cartridge slot, expansion port and Jumper Pak (Easy, about 45 minutes). Most black screens come from dirty or unseated connections in the cartridge slot or expansion port, and from running without a Jumper Pak or Expansion Pak.

Case

  • Reduce yellowing on a console shell (retrobright) (Careful, about 240 minutes). Retrobrite uses hydrogen peroxide and UV light to lighten yellowed plastic. Vapor methods are described by restorers, but the process needs care.

Cleaning

  • Clean Game Boy and GBA cartridge slots and contacts (Easy, about 15 minutes). A dirty or corroded cartridge connection is the most common reason handhelds refuse to boot a game.
  • Clean up a leaked battery inside a handheld or cartridge (Careful, about 45 minutes). Leaked alkaline cells corrode contacts and traces. Neutralising and cleaning early can save the board.
  • Dust out the fan and refresh thermal paste on a hot console (Careful, about 60 minutes). Years of dust and dried paste make consoles run hot. Cleaning the fan and refreshing the paste is basic maintenance.

Controller

  • Fix or replace a worn N64 controller stick (Easy, about 20 minutes). Try the no-tools re-centring tricks first, then swap the stick assembly if it is truly worn.
  • Repair a frayed or intermittent controller cable (Medium, about 45 minutes). Most cord faults are near the strain relief at the controller or console end. Trim back to good wire and resolder.
  • Repair a GameCube controller: dead buttons, drift and worn caps (Medium, about 60 minutes). Carbon contacts and stick modules wear. Cleaning the contacts and replacing the stick fixes most worn controllers.

Diagnosis

  • Dead console triage: no lights, no picture, no boot (Easy, about 45 minutes). This flow separates outside problems, such as power, cables and accessories, from real board faults, so you know when to stop.
  • Game Boy has sound in headphones but not the speaker (Medium, about 45 minutes). The headphone jack contains a switch that cuts the speaker when you plug in. If it sticks, the console thinks headphones are always connected.
  • Genesis or Mega Drive black screen with the power light on (Medium, about 45 minutes). Work from the cheapest checks, a dirty cartridge connector, up to power rails and board damage. Most cases end at the first or second step.
  • PS3 yellow light: heat, thermal paste and the NEC/TOKIN capacitors (Expert, about 180 minutes). The yellow light of death has more than one cause. A full teardown, fresh thermal paste and replacing the NEC/TOKIN capacitors is one documented fix.
  • SNES and Super Famicom will not power on (Careful, about 60 minutes). Start with the cheap external checks, then look at the reverse-polarity diode and decoupling capacitors that commonly kill the power path. Board references are for specific revisions, so match them to your board.

Display

  • Brighten a GBA or GBA SP screen: identify your model first (Careful, about 90 minutes). GBA screens differ by model and by board revision, so check yours before buying a replacement. IPS kits exist but quality varies.

Optical

  • Disc will not read: disc, lens, mechanism or laser? (Careful, about 60 minutes). A stepwise flow so you do not replace a laser that was fine. Cheap steps first, laser last.
  • Dreamcast disc not reading: clean the lens, then tune the laser pot (Careful, about 60 minutes). A dirty lens or a weak laser are common causes of disc read problems. Clean first and tune the laser pot only in tiny, reversible steps.
  • GameCube will not read discs: lens, drive and optical board (Careful, about 90 minutes). Before blaming the laser, check the disc, the lens and the motor. Restorers often find the optical board capacitors are the real culprit.
  • PS1 disc read errors: lens clean, drive check and laser pot (Careful, about 60 minutes). A dirty lens or worn mechanism is the easy fix. If that does not work, mark and tune the laser pot in tiny steps and revert if you hear rubbing.
  • Xbox 360 will not read discs: belt, lens and laser service (Careful, about 120 minutes). A clogged drive, a warped belt and a tired laser can all stop a 360 from reading. A full service with cleaning and a belt or laser replacement fixed one documented unit.

Other

  • Play imported games: Genesis and Mega Drive region lock (Easy, about 15 minutes). Genesis region locking has a software layer and a physical layer. Knowing which one you hit tells you whether an adapter, a Game Genie or a console mod is the right answer.
  • Remove or replace the original Xbox clock capacitor (Careful, about 90 minutes). Early Xboxes have a clock supercapacitor that can leak alkaline fluid and corrode traces. Removing it early is the best fix, and repairing any rotted traces comes second.
  • Swap or upgrade the original Xbox hard drive (Careful, about 60 minutes). The stock Xbox hard drive is locked to the console by a unique key. A swap means cloning the drive and locking the new one correctly.

Power

  • Match, test and swap a console power adapter safely (Easy, about 15 minutes). Most no-power problems start at the wall adapter and its plug, not the console. This flow rules the adapter in or out without opening anything that carries mains voltage.
  • PS1 will not turn on: voltage check, power switch and power jack (Careful, about 75 minutes). Check the supply's mains rating, then clean the power switch and reflow cracked power jack joints, all with the unit unplugged.
  • PS2 will not power on: internal power supply checks (Expert, about 90 minutes). The internal supply is a common failure point. Work only with the console unplugged and stop if live testing is needed.

Video

  • No picture, fuzzy picture or jailbars: check the AV chain (Easy, about 30 minutes). Many video faults are in the cable, port or TV input rather than the console. Work through them in order before opening anything.
